PM Surya Ghar subsidy: the complete 2026 guide

The scheme pays up to ₹78,000 towards a residential rooftop solar system. The structure is simple; the process is where people lose months. Here is both, in order.

Exactly what the scheme pays

PM Surya Ghar: Muft Bijli Yojana is a central subsidy for grid-connected rooftop solar on residential properties. It replaced the older percentage-based rooftop programme with a flat banded structure, which makes it considerably easier to calculate and considerably harder for a salesperson to misrepresent.

Capacity bandRateCumulative subsidy
First 1 kW₹30,000/kW₹30,000
Second kW₹30,000/kW₹60,000
Third kW₹18,000₹78,000
Fourth kW and beyond₹0₹78,000 (capped)

So a 2 kW system attracts ₹60,000, a 3 kW system attracts ₹78,000, and a 10 kW system attracts — still ₹78,000. The cap is per household connection, not per kilowatt.

Several states add their own top-up on the same installation. Delhi and Uttar Pradesh are the notable ones. Our subsidy calculator applies both and gives you the combined figure.

Why 3 kW is the default size — and when to ignore that

The subsidy stops at exactly 3 kW, and that single fact has shaped the entire Indian residential rooftop market. At 3 kW the subsidy covers roughly 45% of project cost. At 10 kW it covers about 17%. Effective subsidy per kilowatt collapses from ₹30,000 on the first to ₹7,800 averaged over ten.

This produces a strong gravitational pull towards 3 kW systems, and for a household consuming 350–400 units a month that is genuinely the right answer. But sizing to the subsidy rather than to your consumption is a mistake in two directions.

If you consume far less than 3 kW would generate — say under 200 units a month — you are installing capacity that exports at a discounted rate for 25 years. A 2 kW system at ₹60,000 subsidy may be the better economic fit even though it leaves ₹18,000 of subsidy unclaimed.

If you consume far more — 700 units a month or above — the kilowatts beyond the third still pay back on their own merits. Unsubsidised solar at ₹40,000–48,000 per kW, displacing electricity at ₹8 a unit, pays back in roughly seven to eight years. That is a worse return than the subsidised portion, but it is still a considerably better return than most places you could park the money. Do not let the subsidy cliff talk you out of a correctly-sized system.

The nine eligibility conditions

  1. Residential consumer. The connection must be a domestic one. Commercial, industrial and agricultural connections are not covered by this scheme.
  2. Connection in the applicant's name. This is the condition that catches the most people. The subsidy is credited to the person whose name is on the electricity connection, not the person who paid the installer.
  3. Grid-connected with a net meter. Off-grid systems and battery-only backup installations are not eligible, regardless of how much solar is involved.
  4. Vendor registered on the National Portal. The installer must be empanelled before the installation. Installing first and finding an empanelled vendor afterwards does not work.
  5. ALMM-listed modules with DCR cells. Modules must be from the Approved List of Models and Manufacturers, using domestic content requirement cells. Imported modules that are cheaper on paper make the installation ineligible.
  6. One subsidy per household connection.
  7. Inspection and commissioning completed. The DISCOM must inspect and the net meter must be commissioned, with both recorded on the portal.
  8. Bank account matching the applicant. The account on the portal must belong to the applicant; mismatches stall the transfer.
  9. Claim filed through the portal. The subsidy is not released against a vendor's invoice alone.

The application, step by step

  1. Register on the national portal at pmsuryaghar.gov.in. You will need your state, your DISCOM and your consumer number — all printed on your electricity bill — plus a mobile number for OTP verification.
  2. Submit the rooftop solar application with your proposed capacity. Your DISCOM receives it for technical feasibility approval.
  3. Wait for feasibility approval. The DISCOM confirms your connection can support the proposed capacity and that net metering is available on your feeder. This is the first real waiting point and it sits entirely with your distribution company.
  4. Choose an empanelled vendor from the portal's registered list for your state, and sign a contract. This is where you should already have three benchmarked quotes in hand — our quote service exists precisely for this step.
  5. Installation. Typically two to four days of work for a residential system.
  6. Submit plant details and apply for the net meter through the portal.
  7. DISCOM inspection and net-meter installation. The second real waiting point, again with your DISCOM.
  8. Commissioning certificate is generated on the portal once inspection passes.
  9. Submit bank details and the subsidy claim. Payment is credited directly to the account.

How long it really takes

The portal indicates the subsidy is credited within about 30 days of commissioning. That is broadly accurate for the payment step itself. What it does not tell you is that the payment step is the last of nine, and that two earlier steps — feasibility approval and net-meter installation — run on your DISCOM's timetable.

A realistic end-to-end expectation, from registration to money in your account, is two to four months in a well-run urban DISCOM area and longer where net-meter supply is constrained. Plan your finances around that, not around the 30-day figure.

One practical consequence: do not borrow on the assumption that the subsidy will arrive before your first EMI. Borrow against the full cost and prepay when it lands. Our EMI calculator handles this.

If a vendor blames the central scheme for a delay, check

The two long poles in this process are both DISCOM steps. A vendor telling you the subsidy portal is slow, when the application has been sitting at feasibility approval for six weeks, is misdirecting you. You can see the current stage yourself on the portal — log in and look rather than relying on the installer's account of it.

Six mistakes that void a claim

  • Installing before portal approval. The sequence matters. An installation done ahead of feasibility approval may not be regularised.
  • Using a non-empanelled installer because the quote was ₹20,000 cheaper. That saving costs you ₹78,000.
  • Non-ALMM or non-DCR modules. Ask for the model number and check it on the ALMM list yourself. Do not accept a verbal assurance.
  • Connection in a parent's or spouse's name while the application is in yours. Either transfer the connection first or apply in the connection-holder's name.
  • Bank account mismatch — a joint account where the applicant is the second holder, or a name spelled differently from the portal record.
  • Skipping the net meter because the vendor says it can be arranged later. Without a commissioned net meter there is no commissioning certificate, and without that there is no subsidy.

Housing societies and RWAs

Group Housing Societies and Resident Welfare Associations have a separate provision: ₹18,000 per kW for common facilities — lifts, water pumps, corridor and compound lighting, EV charging — up to 500 kW, computed at 3 kW per house.

This is materially underused, and the economics are often better than for any individual flat owner in the same building. Common-area load is steady, daytime, and frequently billed at a higher commercial or bulk-supply tariff. A society with a large clear terrace and a heavy lift and pump load can see a payback comparable to a well-sited individual installation, across a much larger system.

The obstacle is rarely financial. It is that a society needs a resolution, a managing committee willing to run a tender, and agreement on who owns the asset. Societies that get past that usually find the numbers straightforward.

Frequently asked

The subsidy applies to new installations processed through the portal. A system already installed and commissioned outside the scheme cannot be retrospectively claimed. Adding capacity to an existing system is a question for your DISCOM, and the answer varies by state.

A capital subsidy towards a residential asset is not ordinary income, but the treatment depends on your circumstances and how the asset is held. Ask a chartered accountant rather than an installer — this is exactly the kind of question where a salesperson's confident answer is worth nothing.

Some empanelled vendors offer to adjust the subsidy against the invoice and claim it themselves, which saves you the wait. It is legitimate, but you are handing them your claim. Get in writing what happens if the claim is rejected for a reason within their control — non-ALMM modules, a botched commissioning — and make sure that risk does not land back on you.

Ask for the reason in writing. The common grounds are transformer capacity constraints on your feeder, or a proposed capacity exceeding your sanctioned load. The first may resolve as the DISCOM upgrades infrastructure; the second you can often fix by applying for a load enhancement, or by reducing the proposed system size. If you believe the refusal is unjustified, the Consumer Grievance Redressal Forum for your state is the escalation route.
