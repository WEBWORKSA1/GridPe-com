What rooftop solar really costs in India, kW by kW

Installed price bands you can hold a quote against, why a 1 kW system costs nearly twice as much per watt as a 10 kW one, and the four places where a quote quietly gets padded.

GPGridPe Research Desk·12 min read·Updated 2026

The price bands

These are all-in installed costs for a grid-tied residential system with ALMM-listed modules, a standard galvanised structure, a net meter and commissioning. They are ranges because installed cost genuinely varies by roof type, structure height, cable runs and brand — anyone quoting a single national number is guessing.

Why small systems cost more per watt

A large part of a rooftop installation is fixed regardless of size: the net-meter application and liaison, a site visit, an installation crew for a day or two, earthing, a lightning arrester, the AC distribution box, and the trip to your DISCOM office. Spread across 1,000 watts these are punishing; across 10,000 watts they nearly disappear.

The variable costs — modules, mounting structure, DC cable — scale roughly linearly. This is why the per-watt curve falls steeply from 1 kW to 5 kW and then flattens. It is also why an installer quoting the same ₹/W for a 1 kW job as for a 10 kW job is either losing money on the small one or overcharging on the large one.

The four line items where quotes get padded

1. The structure

This is the single most common place to hide margin, because it is the component a homeowner is least able to evaluate. A quote should state the structure height, the material and the galvanisation — hot-dip galvanised is the standard; pre-galvanised or painted MS is cheaper and will not last 25 years on an Indian terrace. A high elevated structure is a legitimate and worthwhile extra cost if you want to keep using the terrace; it is not legitimate as an unexplained lump sum.

2. "Premium" modules

The real differences between module tiers are efficiency per square foot, temperature coefficient, warranty term and the likelihood the manufacturer still exists in fifteen years to honour it. Those matter. What does not justify a large premium is marketing language about the cells. If roof space is not your binding constraint, a well-warranted mainstream module at standard efficiency is usually the better value — you are buying kilowatt-hours, not watts per square foot.

3. Net-meter liaison

Frequently quoted as included, then billed as an extra once the process turns out to involve several visits to the DISCOM office. Get it in writing: does the quoted price include the net-meter application, follow-up, inspection coordination and the meter charge itself?

4. The AMC

The first year is usually free. Years two to five are where an AMC can quietly double in price. Cleaning and an annual inspection are not complicated work; ₹500 per kW per year is a reasonable benchmark. A quote bundling five years of AMC at several times that is using the maintenance line to recover margin on the headline price.

The nine things to compare, not just the total

Module brand, model number and wattage · whether cells are DCR (required for subsidy) · inverter brand and warranty years · structure height and galvanisation grade · DC and AC cable gauge · whether net-meter liaison and charges are included · performance warranty terms · AMC price from year two · payment schedule and what triggers each instalment. Two quotes ₹40,000 apart almost always differ on three of these.

Battery: the question people ask too early

Adding storage costs roughly ₹35,000–60,000 per usable kWh installed, it attracts no central subsidy, and it does not improve your financial return. What it buys is running through a power cut.

That is a real benefit in areas with frequent or long outages, and if you are currently running a diesel generator or replacing an inverter-and-battery setup anyway, the comparison is favourable. But treat it as a separate purchase decision with its own justification — reliability — rather than folding it into the solar payback calculation, where it will only make the numbers look worse.

Financing

Residential rooftop solar up to 10 kW is classified as priority-sector lending in India, and public-sector banks typically quote 7–10.5%. An unsecured personal loan at 14–18% changes the arithmetic materially and is rarely the right instrument for this.

The question worth answering is not "what is the interest rate" but "does the monthly saving exceed the EMI". At a 3 kW system saving roughly ₹2,800–3,200 a month, a five-year loan on a ₹80,000 net outlay at 8.5% produces an EMI around ₹1,640 — comfortably cash-flow positive from month one. Run your own numbers in the EMI calculator.

What a fair quote looks like

Divide the total by the watts. If a 3 kW quote comes in at ₹1.65 lakh, that is ₹55/W — squarely inside the normal band. At ₹2.10 lakh it is ₹70/W, which is high for that size and needs an explanation: a tall structure, a difficult roof, premium modules, or margin. Any of those may be justified. What matters is that the installer can tell you which.

The other end is worth equal suspicion. A 3 kW quote at ₹1.20 lakh is ₹40/W, which is below what the components cost at that scale. Something is being left out — usually the structure grade, the cable gauge, or the net-meter work.
