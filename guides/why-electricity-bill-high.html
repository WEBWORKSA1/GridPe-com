Twelve reasons your electricity bill is higher than it should be

Ordered by how often each one turns out to be the answer, and how easy it is to check. Work down the list; most people find the culprit in the first five.

GPGridPe Research Desk·10 min read·Updated 2026
Start with two numbers from your bill

Units consumed and total amount. Divide the second by the first — that is your effective rate. Then put both into the bill calculator with your DISCOM selected. If the calculator's figure is close to your bill, the problem is consumption (reasons 1–7). If your bill is materially higher than the calculator says it should be, the problem is billing (reasons 8–12).

Consumption problems

1. You crossed a slab

Indian tariffs step up steeply. In Maharashtra's MSEDCL area, units 101–300 cost more than twice the first 100, and above 300 the rate climbs again. A summer month that pushes you from 280 to 320 units does not raise your bill by 14% — the marginal units are billed at the highest rate you have reached. In the handful of flat-rate states, crossing a boundary can re-price your entire consumption. Check which slab you landed in this month versus last.

2. Air conditioning

The single largest summer driver in most homes. A 1.5-ton three-star inverter unit draws around 1,300 W; six hours a night for a month is about 234 units. Each degree lower on the thermostat raises consumption by roughly 6%, so running at 20°C instead of 24°C costs about a quarter more. The fix is usually the setting, not the machine.

3. The storage geyser

The single largest winter driver. A 2,000–2,500 W element reheats the tank continuously while switched on, whether or not anyone is using hot water. A geyser left on all day can burn 150+ units a month. A plug-in timer or the habit of switching it off at the board after the morning is the highest-value five-minute fix in an Indian home.

4. An old refrigerator

It runs every hour of every year. A twelve-year-old non-inverter unit can draw two to three times a current five-star model. Also check the door seal — close the door on a sheet of paper; if it slides out easily, cold air is leaking and the compressor is working harder to compensate.

5. The inverter on float

A backup inverter keeping its battery topped up draws 40–70 W around the clock — 30–50 units a month spent on standby. Older units and ageing batteries are the worst offenders. If your supply is reliable, the backup may be costing more than the outages it protects against.

6. A water pump running on a leak

A 1 HP pump draws about 750 W. If a float valve has failed or there is a leak in the overhead tank or line, the pump cycles far more than it should. Check the meter reading at night when nothing should be running — if the meter disc is still turning, something is.

7. More people, more heat, more devices

Guests, a family member working from home, a new appliance, a new EV. Obvious in retrospect and easy to miss month to month. The appliance calculator builds your load profile so you can see where the new units are going.

Billing problems

8. Your sanctioned load is too high

Fixed charges are levied on sanctioned load, not consumption. If your load was upgraded years ago for equipment you no longer run, you are paying a fixed charge on capacity you never use. Check your bill for "maximum demand" over the last twelve months. If your peak never approaches your sanction, apply to your DISCOM for a reduction — one of the few genuinely free savings available.

9. The fuel surcharge moved

Fuel and power purchase cost adjustment is a percentage of your energy charge that the DISCOM revises periodically. In some areas it has run at roughly a third of the energy charge. It can change your bill materially without any change in your consumption, and it is the line on the bill nobody reads.

10. An estimated or average bill

If the meter reader could not access the meter, you may have been billed on an estimate — usually marked "average", "provisional" or "RNA" (reading not available). The next actual reading reconciles it, which can produce a very large bill after several estimated months. Check the reading type on each bill.

11. A meter reading or meter fault

Compare the reading on your bill against the meter itself. Transcription errors happen. If the meter reading is right but consumption still looks implausible, you can request a meter test from your DISCOM — ask for the result in writing. A faulty meter is uncommon but not rare.

12. Arrears, penalties or a lost subsidy

A late-payment surcharge, arrears from a previous period, or a state free-unit subsidy that has been withdrawn because you crossed the eligibility threshold or failed to re-register. In several states, exceeding the free-unit limit by a single unit removes the entire subsidy for that month — a very sharp cliff that looks like a billing error but is not.

If you still cannot explain it

Raise a complaint with your DISCOM and keep the complaint number — the entire escalation route depends on it. If the response is unsatisfactory, the next step is your state's Consumer Grievance Redressal Forum, and after that the Electricity Ombudsman. The DISCOM directory lists every official contact. The national complaint short code is 1912.

Bill scams are common

A message warning your power will be cut tonight unless you call a number and install an app is a scam. DISCOMs do not disconnect over WhatsApp and do not ask you to install remote-access software. Pay only through official channels.

The permanent fix

Every item above is about using less or being billed correctly. The structural alternative is generating your own. At current tariffs, a correctly-sized rooftop system with the PM Surya Ghar subsidy typically pays back in two and a half to five years (faster in sunny, high-tariff states), and then produces close to free electricity for another twenty. Whether it works for you depends on your roof, your tariff and your consumption — the solar calculator tells you in a minute.
