EV vs petrol in India: the real per-kilometre maths

Electric cars are dramatically cheaper to run — under specific conditions. Here is the arithmetic with the flattering assumptions removed, and a straight answer on who those conditions exclude.

GPGridPe Research Desk·11 min read·Updated 2026

The two formulas

Petrol ₹/km = price per litre ÷ real km per litre

EV ₹/km = tariff per unit ÷ (real km per kWh × charging efficiency)

The first is familiar. The second contains two corrections that most published comparisons skip, and they are worth about 35–45% of the EV figure between them.

Correction one: real efficiency, not certified range

Indian EVs are certified on the MIDC cycle: a laboratory run at modest speeds, mild temperature, no air conditioning, no passengers. Dividing certified range by battery capacity gives an efficiency number that no owner will see. Independent road tests consistently land 20–35% below it. A Nexon EV with a certified 489 km on a 45 kWh pack implies about 10.9 km/kWh; tested, it delivers about 7.8.

Correction two: charging losses

Not every unit drawn from the wall reaches the battery. AC charging through the car's onboard charger loses roughly 12%; DC fast charging, which bypasses it, loses around 6%. So the effective efficiency is real km/kWh multiplied by 0.88 on AC and 0.94 on DC.

A worked comparison

Tata Nexon EV 45 at a tested 7.79 km/kWh, against a 15 km/l petrol hatchback, in Delhi at ₹102.12 a litre:

Scenario₹ per kmAnnual (15,000 km)
Petrol, 15 km/l₹6.81₹1,02,120
EV, public DC at ₹21/unit₹2.87₹43,050
EV, 80% home / 20% public₹1.51₹22,650
EV, all home at ₹8/unit₹1.17₹17,550

On home charging the saving is about ₹84,600 a year. On public fast charging alone it falls to about ₹59,000. Both are real money. The gap between them is what determines whether an EV premium pays back inside a normal ownership period.

Break-even: how much you need to drive

An EV typically costs ₹2–4 lakh more than a comparable petrol car before any incentive. At a ₹3 lakh premium and home charging saving ₹5.64 per km, you need roughly 53,000 km to recover the premium on fuel alone.

  • At 15,000 km a year that is about three and a half years.
  • At 8,000 km a year it is closer to seven years — roughly the whole period many owners keep a car.
  • On public charging only, the break-even moves out by about 40%.

Maintenance adds to the EV's advantage — no oil changes, filters, clutch or exhaust — and typically saves several thousand rupees a year. Insurance runs the other way: EV premiums are higher, reflecting a higher insured value and costlier repairs. The two roughly offset for most mainstream models, which is why this guide treats fuel as the decisive variable.

Where the ₹1/km claim comes from

It is roughly true for a small, efficient EV charged at home on a low tariff. An MG Comet at 11.17 km/kWh on an ₹8 tariff with 88% efficiency comes to ₹0.81/km. A Tiago EV at 7.77 km/kWh comes to ₹1.17. A Mahindra BE 6 at 5.68 km/kWh comes to ₹1.60 at home and about ₹3.93 on public DC.

The claim is not dishonest, but it describes the best case, and the spread across the market is roughly five-fold depending on which car and which charger. Use the EV database to check the specific model you are considering.

When an EV does not pay

  • You cannot charge at home. This is the decisive factor, and it is binary. An apartment resident without a dedicated parking bay and the society's permission to install a charger will be on public charging, and at 2.5–3x the home rate the economics become marginal for a low-mileage driver.
  • You drive under about 8,000 km a year. The fuel saving is too small to recover a meaningful premium in a normal ownership period.
  • You are replacing a CNG car. At ₹76–95 per kg and 25–30 km/kg, CNG runs at ₹2.60–3.50 per km — close to public-charging EV cost and within ₹1.50/km of home charging. The saving is real but small.
  • You do long highway runs regularly. Consumption rises sharply above 90 km/h, fast-charging infrastructure on many corridors is still thin, and you will be paying DC rates for most of those kilometres.
  • You are in a high electricity slab. At ₹11 a unit rather than ₹8, home charging cost rises by 37%. Check whether your state offers a separate EV tariff on a dedicated meter; several do.

The resale question

This is the largest unknown in Indian EV ownership, and anyone quoting a confident five-year residual value is guessing. The Indian used-EV market is young, battery health is hard for a buyer to verify, and rapid improvements in new models depress the value of older ones. A conservative approach is to assume steeper depreciation than an equivalent petrol car and see whether the fuel saving still carries the decision. If it does, the resale question becomes upside rather than risk.

Solar changes the equation completely

A 3 kW rooftop system generates roughly 12 units a day — enough for about 90 km of EV driving. If you have both, the marginal fuel cost of daytime-charged kilometres approaches zero, and the combined payback on the two investments is considerably better than either alone. Run the solar calculator with your EV's consumption added to your bill.

The five-minute test before a showroom visit

  1. Find the model's tested km/kWh in the EV database.
  2. Decide honestly what share of your charging will happen at home.
  3. Enter both, plus your real annual kilometres, into the EV calculator.
  4. Divide the price premium over the petrol equivalent by the annual saving.
  5. If the answer is under your intended ownership period, the EV pays. If it is not, the decision has to rest on something other than running cost — which is a perfectly legitimate place for it to rest, as long as you know that is what you are doing.
