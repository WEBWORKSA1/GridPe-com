Installing a home EV charger: cost, wiring and load

Most Indian EV owners do not need the wallbox they are sold. Some do. Here is how to tell which one you are, and what the installation should actually involve.

GPGridPe Research Desk·9 min read·Updated 2026

Figures assume the car can accept the charger's full rate. Several popular models accept only 3.3 kW on AC, in which case a 7.2 kW wallbox charges them no faster than the portable cable. Check your car's maximum AC rate in the EV database before buying anything.

Who actually needs the wallbox

The 3.3 kW portable covers 250–300 km overnight. The average Indian urban car covers well under 60 km a day. For most owners, the portable cable is not a compromise — it is sufficient with a large margin.

A 7.2 kW wallbox earns its cost in three situations:

  • You drive more than about 120 km a day regularly, so overnight on 3.3 kW would not reliably restore the battery.
  • You have a large battery — 60 kWh and above — where a full charge on 3.3 kW would take more than eighteen hours.
  • Two EVs share one parking spot and need to charge in sequence overnight.

If none of those describe you, start with the portable cable. You can always add a wallbox later; you cannot un-spend ₹28,000.

The load question

This is the part installers and dealers tend to underplay. A 7.2 kW charger running at full rate draws 7.2 kW continuously for hours. If your sanctioned load is 5 kW and the air conditioner, geyser and refrigerator are running, you will exceed it — and depending on your meter and DISCOM, that means tripping, a penalty for exceeding contract demand, or both.

Before installing a wallbox, add 7.2 kW to your realistic evening peak and compare it to your sanctioned load. If it exceeds, apply to your DISCOM for a load enhancement. This costs a fee plus a higher fixed charge every month for the life of the connection, which should be counted in the charger's real cost.

A smart charger can avoid the upgrade

Some wallboxes support load management — they monitor your household consumption and throttle the charge rate to stay under your sanctioned limit. If you are near the threshold, this can be cheaper than a permanent load enhancement and its recurring fixed charge.

What the wiring should involve

Whether portable or wallbox, the charging point should be on a dedicated circuit from your distribution board, not a shared one. A licensed electrician should install:

  • An appropriately rated MCB for the circuit.
  • An RCD or RCBO — residual current protection is essential for EV charging, and a Type B or equivalent DC-leakage-sensitive device is recommended for many chargers. Ask what the charger manufacturer specifies.
  • Copper cable sized for the continuous current and the length of the run, with allowance for voltage drop on longer runs.
  • Proper earthing, tested. Poor earthing is the most common defect in Indian EV charging installations.
  • For an outdoor or parking-area location, a weatherproof enclosure.

Do not charge an EV from an ordinary 6 A socket, an extension cord or a multi-plug adapter. EV charging draws high current continuously for hours, which is a quite different load from any household appliance, and undersized wiring can overheat.

Apartments and housing societies

The hard part in an apartment is rarely technical. It is permission and metering. Your society needs to agree to the installation in a common or allocated parking area, and the charger needs to be connected either to your own flat's meter — which may mean a long cable run — or to a separate meter. Several states' EV policies and model bye-laws support residents' right to install a charger in their allotted parking. Put the request to your society in writing, reference your state policy, and offer to bear the cost.

Should you apply for an EV tariff?

Several states offer a separate EV charging tariff on a dedicated meter, often around ₹5.50–8 per unit, compared with a domestic slab rate that may be considerably higher. There is a secondary benefit: charging on a separate meter keeps EV consumption out of your household total, so it does not push your domestic consumption into a higher slab.

It usually requires a second meter and the associated application and fixed charge. If you charge daily and your domestic slab rate is high, it is typically worth doing. If you charge twice a week and sit in a low slab, it probably is not. Run your numbers in the charging calculator with both rates.

What to ask before signing a quote

  1. Is this a dedicated circuit from the distribution board?
  2. What MCB and RCD rating and type, and does that match the charger manufacturer's specification?
  3. What cable size and length?
  4. Will the earthing be tested, and will I get the reading?
  5. Does my sanctioned load need enhancing — and if you say no, show me the arithmetic.
  6. Is the electrician licensed, and is the installation covered by any warranty?
