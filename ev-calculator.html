What this excludes

Fuel only. EVs also cut servicing costs materially (no oil, filters, clutch or exhaust) but carry higher insurance premiums and a steeper depreciation curve in the Indian resale market. Battery replacement outside warranty is the tail risk — priced at roughly ₹5,000–8,000 per kWh today and falling.

The formula, spelled out

Cost per kilometre for a petrol car is trivial: fuel price divided by real mileage. For an EV there is a step people miss — charging losses. Pushing 10 units into the wall socket does not put 10 units into the battery.

Petrol ₹/km = petrol price ÷ km per litre

EV ₹/km = tariff per unit ÷ (real km per kWh × charging efficiency)

We use 88% efficiency for AC home charging and 94% for DC fast charging — DC bypasses the onboard charger and loses less. Ignoring this understates EV running cost by roughly 12%, which is how a lot of published comparisons end up flattering.

Worked example

A Tata Nexon EV 45 tested at 7.79 km/kWh, charged at home on an ₹8/unit tariff:

8 ÷ (7.79 × 0.88) = ₹1.17 per km

On public DC at ₹21/unit: 21 ÷ (7.79 × 0.94) = ₹2.87 per km

Against a 15 km/l petrol hatchback in Delhi at ₹102.12/litre: 102.12 ÷ 15 = ₹6.81 per km

At 15,000 km a year, home charging saves about ₹84,600 annually. Running entirely on public fast charging, the saving falls to roughly ₹59,000 — still substantial, but it pushes the payback on a ₹3 lakh premium from three and a half years to over five.

The four things that change the answer

  1. Whether you can charge at home. This is the single biggest variable and it is binary. A dedicated parking spot with a socket you control is worth more to EV economics than any specification on the brochure. Apartment dwellers without a committed bay should model this at 0% home charging and see whether the numbers still work.
  2. Annual mileage. Below about 8,000 km a year, the fuel saving is too small to repay a price premium inside the ownership period. EVs reward high-mileage drivers; they punish weekend cars.
  3. Your electricity tariff. If you are in a high slab at ₹11/unit, home charging costs half again as much as it does at ₹7. Several states offer a separate, cheaper EV tariff on a dedicated meter — worth applying for if you are charging daily.
  4. What you are actually replacing. Comparing an EV to a 9 km/l diesel SUV yields a spectacular saving. Comparing it to a CNG hatchback at ₹2.60/km yields almost none. Be honest about the alternative.
The claim to distrust

"₹1 per kilometre" is the standard EV marketing line. It holds only on home charging, on the most efficient models, at a low tariff, with charging losses ignored. It is roughly true for an MG Comet at 11.17 km/kWh on a ₹8 tariff. It is not true for a 79 kWh SUV at 5.68 km/kWh, which lands closer to ₹1.60 on the same tariff — and near ₹4 on public fast charging.
