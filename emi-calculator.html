The EMI formula

EMI = P × r × (1+r)n ÷ ((1+r)n − 1)

Where P is the principal, r is the monthly rate (annual rate ÷ 1200) and n is the number of months. Nothing exotic — but two things about solar loans specifically are worth understanding.

The tenure trade-off is unusually clean here

With most loans, stretching the tenure is straightforwardly bad: you pay more interest for a lower instalment. With solar it is more interesting, because the asset generates a fixed monthly saving. A three-year tenure often produces an EMI that exceeds the saving, which means you fund the gap from your pocket. A seven-year tenure usually produces an EMI comfortably below the saving — you pay more interest in total, but you are never out of pocket in any month, and the system is generating free electricity for eighteen years after the loan closes.

Which is right depends on whether you value total cost or monthly cash flow. If you have the cash, paying outright always wins on total cost — a ₹1.2 lakh system at 8.5% over five years costs roughly ₹27,500 in interest, which is real money against a 25-year saving. If the alternative to borrowing is not installing at all, borrow.

Watch the processing fee and the collateral question

A 1–2% processing fee on a small loan is a meaningful addition to the effective rate. And note that a solar loan may be offered either as a secured loan against the asset or folded into a top-up on a home loan. The home-loan route usually carries the lowest rate available to you, but it extends the debt over a much longer horizon than the equipment's life — you can end up paying for an inverter in year 18 that was replaced in year 12.

Do not borrow against a subsidy you have not received

The PM Surya Ghar subsidy arrives after installation, inspection and net-meter commissioning — a process that runs on your DISCOM's timetable, not yours. Borrow against the full cost and plan to prepay when the subsidy lands, rather than assuming it will be in your account before the first instalment. Confirm your lender permits partial prepayment without penalty; on a floating-rate retail loan to an individual, it generally must.
